Two Light Flashes from a Moving Source

A spacecraft moves to the right at velocity v relative to the ground. At time t′ = 0 in the spacecraft frame, an observer at the centre of the ship flashes light simultaneously in both directions. Detectors are placed at equal distances on each end of the ship.

Question: Does the light reach both detectors at the same time?

Answer depends on the frame:

  • Spacecraft frame (top): both detectors are stationary, equidistant. Light travels at c in both directions. Both detectors fire simultaneously at t′ = L/c.
  • Ground frame (bottom): both detectors move to the right with the ship. Light still travels at c in both directions (Einstein’s 2nd postulate). But the back detector moves toward the back-going photon, while the front detector moves away from the front-going photon. The back detector fires before the front detector.

This is the relativity of simultaneity: events simultaneous in one inertial frame are not simultaneous in another moving relative to it. The same physical events have different time-orderings in different frames.

Key formulas:
  • Ship-frame strike time (both detectors): t′ = L/c  (simultaneous)
  • Ground-frame back-detector strike: tback = L / (γ (c + v))
  • Ground-frame front-detector strike: tfront = L / (γ (c − v))
  • Asymmetry: Δt = tfront − tback = 2 L v γ / c²

Both photons in the ground frame still travel at c (you can check: each photon position in the ground frame is just ±c·t). What differs between frames is when the moving detectors meet the photons.
